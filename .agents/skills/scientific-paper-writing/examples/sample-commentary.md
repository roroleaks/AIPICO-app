# Sample Scientific Commentary Paper

# Vaginal Progesterone versus Cervical Cerclage for the Prevention of Preterm Birth in Singleton Gestations with a Short Cervix: An Evidence-Based Commentary

**Keywords:** Premature Birth / prevention & control, Uterine Cervical Incompetence / therapy, Progesterone / therapeutic use, Cerclage, Cervical / adverse effects, Pregnancy, High-Risk

---

### Abstract

**Background:** Spontaneous preterm birth remains the leading cause of neonatal mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental morbidity worldwide. Asymptomatic cervical shortening diagnosed by midtrimester transvaginal ultrasound identifies patients at high risk who benefit from targeted intervention.  
**Clinical Question:** In asymptomatic singleton pregnancies with midtrimester cervical length ≤ 25 mm, does vaginal progesterone compare favorably to cervical cerclage in reducing preterm birth before 34 weeks and composite neonatal morbidity?  
**Evidence Synthesis:** Direct evidence from randomized controlled trials and individual patient data meta-analyses demonstrates that vaginal progesterone significantly reduces spontaneous preterm birth before 33 weeks (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.81) and composite neonatal adverse outcomes [1, 2]. Cervical cerclage provides comparable risk reduction in patients with both a short cervix and prior spontaneous preterm birth (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.55–0.89) [2, 3], but fails to demonstrate benefit in unselected singleton pregnancies without prior history [4]. For patients without prior preterm birth, progesterone is superior, avoiding procedural morbidity.  
**Conclusion:** Vaginal progesterone represents the preferred first-line intervention for asymptomatic short cervix in singleton gestations, offering equivalent perinatal efficacy to cerclage without procedural complications. Cerclage remains a viable alternative when cervical shortening progresses despite progesterone therapy in women with prior spontaneous preterm birth.

---

### Introduction

Spontaneous preterm birth before 37 weeks complicates approximately 10% of pregnancies globally and accounts for over 75% of perinatal deaths and significant long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae [1]. Transvaginal ultrasonographic screening of cervical length between 18 and 24 weeks of gestation has revolutionized risk stratification, as a cervical length ≤ 25 mm strongly predicts imminent spontaneous preterm delivery [1, 2].

The management of midtrimester cervical shortening historically centered on surgical cervical cerclage, designed to provide mechanical closure and preserve the mucus plug barrier [3]. Concurrently, the discovery of progesterone deficiency in premature labor led to the clinical adoption of vaginal natural progesterone, which exerts profound anti-inflammatory, tocolytic, and myometrial quiescent effects [1].

Despite extensive investigation, clinical equipoise has persisted regarding whether non-invasive hormonal supplementation achieves parity with surgical cerclage, or if specific clinical phenotypes necessitate surgical intervention [2-4]. This commentary synthesizes direct clinical evidence to provide clear practice recommendations for maternal-fetal medicine specialists and obstetricians.

---

### Discussion

#### Primary Efficacy: Preterm Birth Prevention
High-quality randomized trials and individual patient data meta-analyses confirm that vaginal progesterone reduces preterm birth before 33 weeks by 38% compared to placebo in women with midtrimester cervical length ≤ 25 mm (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.81) [1]. Head-to-head network comparisons demonstrate that vaginal progesterone and cervical cerclage exhibit statistically comparable efficacy in reducing delivery before 34 weeks among women with a prior preterm birth and shortened cervix (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.63–1.23) [2, 3].

#### Neonatal Morbidity and Mortality
Perinatal outcomes parallel the gestational prolongation observed with both interventions. Vaginal progesterone achieves a 35% reduction in composite neonatal morbidity and mortality, including respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, and necrotizing enterocolitis [1]. Cerclage demonstrates similar reductions in neonatal composite morbidity in history-indicated cohorts [3], but does not show additive benefit when combined with progesterone in unselected singleton pregnancies [4].

#### Subgroup Heterogeneity: Prior Preterm Birth vs. Incidental Short Cervix
The critical determinant of comparative efficacy is prior obstetric history. In women with no history of spontaneous preterm birth who present with an incidental short cervix ≤ 25 mm, cervical cerclage does not reduce preterm birth (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.81–1.49) [4]. In stark contrast, vaginal progesterone maintains robust efficacy in this cohort [1]. Conversely, in patients with both a prior spontaneous preterm birth and transvaginal cervical length < 25 mm before 24 weeks, both cerclage and progesterone demonstrate significant reductions in delivery before 34 weeks [2, 3].

#### Safety Profiles and Procedural Morbidity
A major consideration distinguishing these two therapeutic strategies is safety. Vaginal progesterone exhibits a favorable safety profile, with adverse events limited to minor vaginal discharge and local irritation, with no evidence of maternal or fetal toxicity [1]. In contrast, surgical cerclage requires regional or general anesthesia and carries inherent procedural risks, including intraoperative bleeding, cervical laceration, iatrogenic membrane rupture, chorioamnionitis, and suture displacement requiring emergency removal [3, 4].

#### Methodological Quality and Certainty of Evidence
The evidence base for vaginal progesterone is supported by multi-center randomized controlled trials with low risk of bias and high GRADE certainty [1, 2]. The evidence supporting cerclage in history-indicated subgroups demonstrates moderate-to-high certainty, whereas its use in nulliparous or unselected patients is constrained by negative trial results and increased procedural risk [3, 4].

---

### Conclusion

For asymptomatic singleton gestations with transvaginal cervical length ≤ 25 mm, vaginal progesterone (200 mg daily) is the first-line evidence-based standard of care. It confers equivalent preterm birth reduction to cerclage in high-risk patients with prior history, while eliminating the anesthetic and operative risks of surgery. Cerclage should be reserved for singleton pregnancies with prior spontaneous preterm birth that exhibit persistent cervical shortening (< 10 mm) or funneling despite progesterone therapy.

Future trials should focus on biomarkers of cervical extracellular matrix remodeling and individualized pharmacokinetic profiling to optimize dosing and route of administration for patients with extreme cervical shortening.
